Introduction
High-resolution remote sensing (RS) has transformed observation of riverscapes by enabling 
routine, synoptic mapping of water extent, channel morphology, and habitat structure at 
organism-relevant spatial grains and operational cadences (Marcus and Fonstad, 2010). 
Expanding public and commercial image archives, cloud platforms, and open analytical 
ecosystems now support ecological assessment at spatial and temporal resolutions that fineld 
data collection campaigns alone cannot sustain (Cavender-Bares et al., 2022). Field surveys 
remain indispensable yet are constrained by access, safety, and personnel, particularly during 
high floows or across difficcult flooodplain terrain, creating persistent gaps in coverage and 
frequency (Calderon and An, 2016; Gri fficn and Lasko, 2020; Johansen et al., 2022; Suir et al., 
2023).  Deep learning and modern ML offeer stronger representation capacity but remain 
sensitive to label scarcity, class imbalance, and domain shifte across color/turbidity/illumination 
(Aggarwal, 2018; Fix and Hodges, 1989; Ma et al., 2019; Maskey et al., 2018; Vahidi et al., 2023; 
Wang et al., 2019; Wurm et al., 2019). Moreover, ecohydraulic mapping at scale is constrained 
by the cost and data demands of LiDAR-driven or fully calibrated hydraulic models 
(Lindenschmidt and Carr, 2018; Schwartz, 2016). Because mesohabitat composition shi ftes with 
streamfloow, models must respect floow dependence and test temporal generalization (Aadland, 
1993; Harris et al., 2024).

Within hierarchical river habitat frameworks, mesohabitats (e.g., pools, rifflees/glides, 
runs/raceways) occupy the intermediate scale between microhabitats and reaches and exert 
finrst-order control on hydraulics, sediment processes, and aquatic assemblages (Aadland, 1993; 
Wegscheider et al., 2020). Thee spatial confinguration of these mesohabitats shiftes with 
streamfloow and sediment dynamics, highlighting the need for mapping approaches that are 
both spatially extensive and temporally responsive (Van Rooijen and Lotsari, 2024; Zaimes et 
al., 2021). Conventional delineation via expert fineld mapping and ecohydraulic modeling can 
achieve high findelity but is typically limited to short river segments and sparse floow snapshots 
given the demands for detailed topography, boundary conditions, and calibration (Harris et al., 
2024; Schwartz, 2016; Wiest et al., 2024).
Remote sensing provides an opportunity to upscale mesohabitat assessment by exploiting 
spectral, textural, and morphological signals. Habitat studies are increasingly using RS tools 
such as supervised/unsupervised land-cover classifincation, spectral indices (e.g., Normalized 
Diffeerence Vegetation Index [NDVI], Normalized Diffeerence Water Index [NDWI], Normalized

(e.g. slope, curvature) (Demir et al., 2018; Farwell et al., 2021; Ganesh et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2021;

work has leveraged geographic object-based image analysis (OBIA) to map Rriverscape units

Theis study advances riverscape mapping by testing whether mesohabitat classes can be 
inferred directly from high-resolution satellite features across varying discharges, rather than 
relying on physical parameters (e.g. depth and velocity). Using the Big Blue–Kansas Rivers 
conflouence as a case study, the work (i) establishes reach-scale baseline landcover land-use 
(LULC) and mesoscale change products for context and continuity, and (ii) evaluates image-
based prediction of eight hydraulically definned mesohabitats using PlanetScope imagery 
(Planet Labs PBC, 2024) due to its consistent, near-same-day coverage of all modelled dates . 
Thee central objective is to quantify how well satellite-only predictors recover mesohabitat 
labels under temporally out-of-sample conditions and to benchmark those results against the 
simpler inundation classifincation (i.e., binary water versus non-water derived from spectral 
refloectance and indices), delivering categorical prediction maps and cross-date performance 
summaries suitable for monitoring and management. All analyses are implemented with open, 
reusable tooling to maximize transparency and transferability.
Methodology
Theis study employs two complementary classifincation workfloows to evaluate riverscape 
mapping from satellite imagery: (i) a semi-supervised clustering approach (K-means with KNN 
labeling) and (ii) a supervised random forest classifiner. Both methods were applied to satellite 
imagery with diffeerent spatial resolutions to generate landcover and inundation products 
across the full 22-image time series. Theese classifincations were then used to quantify temporal 
dynamics in surface water and exposed land within the river corridor. In a separate analysis, 
image-based predictors derived from PlanetScope imagery were used to directly classify 
hydraulically definned mesohabitats for seven dates corresponding to 2D hydraulic model 
outputs, evaluated using a leave-one-date-out framework to assess temporal generalization 
across floow conditions.

Semi-Supervised Classification
A semi-supervised classifincation approach was used to categorize the imagery into finve classes 
based offe the Anderson land cover classifincation (Anderson et al., 1976): urban (e.g. roads, 
buildings, etc), bare land, herbaceous vegetation (e.g., grasses, agricultural cover), forested land, 
and surface water. Theis approach was applied consistently across sensors to enable direct 
comparison across sensors.
Thee workfloow combined unsupervised clustering with supervised classifincation. K-means 
clustering (Hartigan–Wong algorithm) was finrst used to partition the spectral feature space by 
minimizing within-cluster sum of squares (Hartigan and Wong, 1979). Thee resulting clusters 
were then assigned class labels and used to train a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifiner, which 
predicts class membership for unseen pixels based on the majority class of neighboring 
observations in feature space.
Thee number of clusters (k) was evaluated using both the elbow method and silhouettee analysis. 
Thee elbow method identifines the point of diminishing returns in within-cluster variance 
reduction, while silhouettee analysis evaluates cluster separation by comparing within- and 
between-cluster similarity. Although silhouettee analysis indicated optimal clustering at low k 
values (k = 2–3), these solutions primarily separated broad landcover groups and did not 
capture the level of thematic detail required intended for this study. Theerefore, an elbow at a 
higher values of k (> 5) are preferredwas selected based on a balance between statistical 
performance and interpretability of landcover classes.
Supervised Classification
A supervised classifincation approach was used to assign pixels to the finve landcover classes 
described in the previous section. Landcover classes were manually delineated as vector 
polygons within the AOI using QGIS (QGIS Development Team, 2024) and subsequently 
rasterized to generate labeled training pixels. To reduce temporal noise in the training data, 

===== PAGE 10 =====
only areas that appeared visually stable across the study period (i.e., exhibited no observable 
landcover change between dates) were selected.
Model training was conducted using a random forest classifiner, with 80% of the labeled pixels 
used for training and the remaining 20% reserved for validation. Thee number of trees was finxed 
at 500 for all models. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using finve-fold cross-validation on 
a stratifined subset of the training data, where a maximum of 10,000 samples per class were 
randomly selected to reduce computational demand while preserving class balance. Thee 
optimal model confinguration was selected based on classifincation performance metrics, 
including overall accuracy and Receiver Operating Characteristic Area Under the Curve (ROC 
AUC). Two key hyperparameters were tuned: (1) min_n, the minimum number of observations 
required to split a node, and (2) mtry, the number of predictors randomly sampled at each split.
Thee finnal models were trained at their original spatial resolutions using the full set of labeled 
pixels and the tuned hyperparameters. For the baseline classifincation, predictor variables 
consisted of multi-band raster stacks in which each pixel contained spectral information from 
all image acquisition dates for a given sensor. Theis formulation allows the model to learn a 
temporally integrated representation of surface conditions, producing a classifincation that 
refloects the dominant or persistent landcover state over the study period.
In addition, separate random forest models were trained for each image date using only the 
spectral bands associated with that date. Theese date-specifinc models preserve short-term 
variability in landcover conditions and were used to generate temporally explicit classifined 
rasters. Because the baseline model was trained using a multi-band stack containing predictors 
from all image dates, the tuned mtry refloects a high-dimensional feature space. In contrast, the 
date-specifinc models were trained using only four spectral predictors per date. As a result, mtry 
was constrained to 2 for the date-speci finc models to ensure consistency with the reduced 
predictor set.

To further evaluate relationships between landcover classes, Kendall’s tau (τ) rank correlation 
coefficcient was computed between surface water and bare land area time series. Kendall’s τ is a 
nonparametric measure of monotonic association that is robust to non-normality and small 
sample sizes. It is calculated based on the diffeerence between concordant and discordant pairs 
of observations and ranges from -1 to +1, where negative values indicate an inverse 
relationship, positive values indicate a direct relationship, and values near zero indicate littele or 
no association. In this study, Kendall’s τ was used to assess whether increases in surface water 
correspond to decreases in exposed bare land, as expected under varying floow conditions.
Model Evaluation
Surfae Water Validation (Water vs. Non-Water)
For the water versus non-water validation, a baseline surface-water mask was derived from the 
seven 2D hydraulic model–derived mesohabitat maps by taking the per-pixel median 
mesohabitat state across dates. Thee dates were specifincally chosen to represent a range of 
diverse floow conditions to remove bias, resulting in changing floow volumes and diffeerent 
mesohabitat distributions across time. Pixels within the median mesohabitat footprint were 
classifined as water, whereas pixels outside this footprint were classifined as non-water.
PlanetScope imagery acquired on the same seven dates was co-registered to the hydraulic grid 
and used as input to the semi-supervised and supervised classifincation workfloows. Thee resulting 
classifined products were then evaluated against the baseline mask using confusion matrices. 
Theis analysis was designed to assess whether image-based classifincations could recover the 
spatial extent of surface water consistent with hydraulically derived conditions.
Model performance was summarized using overall accuracy, sensitivity, specifincity, precision, 
negative predictive value, balanced accuracy, Mattehews Correlation Coefficcient (MCC), and F-
measure. Theese metrics were used to evaluate both overall agreement and the ability of each 
model to recover surface water under class imbalance, with particular emphasis on balanced 
accuracy and MCC because they are less sensitive to unequal class frequencies than overall 
accuracy alone.
Mesohabitat Classification
To evaluate whether mesohabitat types can be predicted directly from satellite data, rather 
than inferred indirectly through inundation, 3-m resolution 2D hydraulic model–derived 
mesohabitat distribution maps were used as categorical ground truth, and image-based models 
were trained to predict seven aquatic mesohabitat types described in Section 2.3. PlanetScope 
imagery corresponding to the seven hydraulic model dates specifined in Section 2.3 was co-
registered to the mesohabitat grid.

===== PAGE 12 =====
Predictors were restricted to PlanetScope imagery for this analysis. Landsat-9 and Sentinel-2 
data were not used because they did not provide consistent, cloud-free coverage for all seven 
hydraulic model dates and introduced mixed-pixel effeects at 30 m resolution that are unsuitable 
for resolving narrow channel features. Inclusion of Landsat-9 and Sentinel-2 would have 
required either dropping dates or resampling mesohabitat labels to a lower resolution to match 
the coarser spatial resolution of the two sensors.
A leave-one-date-out (LOO) design was adopted to account for the floow dependence of 
mesohabitats. For each iteration, one date was held out for testing while models were trained 
on the remaining six dates and evaluated on the hold-out. Two modeling approaches were 
compared. Thee finrst approach used semi-supervised clustering. Predictor variables were 
standardized, and K-means clustering was applied to the training pixels (k = 12; nstart = 10). 
Each cluster was assigned a mesohabitat label based on majority voting from the training data, 
and predictions for the held-out date were generated by assigning pixels to the nearest cluster 
center in standardized feature space. Thee second approach used a supervised random forest 
classifiner. A class-weighted model with the same hyperparameters described in Section 2.4.2 
was implemented using the R tidymodels and ranger packages. Class weights were definned as 
inversely proportional to class frequency in the training data to mitigate imbalance. Predictor 
variables included the four spectral bands and three derived indices.
All modeling and evaluation were restricted to the mesohabitat mask (i.e., the river corridor). 
Model performance was summarized for each fold using overall accuracy and class-balanced 
metrics (balanced accuracy and macro-averaged recall), and prediction maps for each held-out 
date were exported as categorical rasters.
Results
Optimized Modeling Parameters
Thee optimized modeling parameters for the unsupervised and semi-supervised methods were 
determined using the elbow method and random forest hyperparameters, respectively. Thee 
elbow method indicated diminishing improvements in within-cluster variance reduction 
beyond k ≈ 6–10 (Fig. 3). Silhouettee analysis identifined k = 6 as the optimal solution, yielding 
the highest average silhouettee width; this value was therefore adopted for subsequent 
modeling. Thee K-means algorithm was run with a maximum of 500 iterations and applied to 
the full image collection to establish baseline landcover conditions. Random forest 
hyperparameter tuning indicated that performance was consistently high across a broad range 
of parameter combinations, with AUC values exceeding 0.99928  for both PlanetScope and 
Landsat-9 datasets (Fig. 4). Model performance was relatively insensitive to small variations in 
both mi nn and mtry, although optimal performance was generally observed for low values of 
mi nn and moderate values of mtry. Thee refinned tuning results (Fig. 4b) show a clear region of 
high performance across both sensors, with the highest AUC values occurring at mtry = 7 and 
low mi nn. Based on these results, the selected confingurations for the baseline models were mi nn 
= 2 and mtry = 7 for PlanetScope, and mi nn = 2 and mtry = 13 for Landsat-9 and Sentinel-2. For 
date-specifinc models for all sensors, mtry was reduced to 2 to re floect the smaller predictor set 
used in those analyses.

Surfae Water Validation
Five land-cover classes were mapped (i.e. urban, bare land, herbaceous vegetation, riparian 
trees/shrubs, and surface water); however, the analysis focused on surface water and bare land 
as complementary indicators of inundation dynamics (Fig. 5–6]). For this analysis, the bare

were included to support classifincation but are not considered further in the validation.
Predicted surface water and bare land extents varied across sensors and modeling approaches 
(Fig. 7). For Landsat-9, surface water spanned approximately 183–390 acres under the semi-
supervised model and 249–329 acres under the supervised model, while bare land ranged from 
372–623 and 268–488 acres, respectively. For Sentinel-2, surface water ranged from 311–386

Thee temporal relationship between surface water and bare land remained inverse across 
sensors and modeling approaches (Figs. 8 , 9). For Landsat-9, Kendall’s tau values were negative 
for both semi-supervised and supervised classifincations, ranging from τ=-0.73 to -0.50. Thee 
semi-supervised model yielded τ=-0.73 for both between-date change and deviations relative to 
baseline, whereas the supervised model produced τ=-0.55 and -0.50, respectively. Sentinel-2 
also showed negative associations for both approaches. Thee semi-supervised model yielded τ
=-0.60 for between-date change and τ=-0.37 relative to baseline, while the supervised model 
produced τ=-0.73 and -0.51, respectively. PlanetScope likewise showed negative relationships 
between surface water and bare land. Thee semi-supervised model yielded τ=-0.58 for between-
date change and τ=-0.49 relative to baseline, whereas the supervised model produced τ=-0.60 
and -0.62, respectively. Across all three sensors, Kendall’s tau remained negative for both 
between-date changes and baseline-relative deviations, indicating an inverse temporal 
relationship between surface water and bare land.
Thee median mesohabitat distribution derived from the seven 2D hydraulic model dates de finned 
the baseline surface-water footprint used for validation (Fig. 11). Confusion-matrix results 
showed that the semi-supervised model identifined more true surface water pixels than the 
supervised model (304 vs. 235), but also produced more false positives (43 vs.  23; Table 3). Thee 
supervised model more frequently classifined non-water correctly (183 vs.  163), but also failed to 
detect a larger number of true water pixels (115 false negatives vs. 46 for the semi-supervised 
model). Consistent with these patteerns, the semi-supervised model achieved higher balanced 
accuracy (0.66) and MCC (0.66) than the supervised model (balanced accuracy = 0.56; MCC = 
0.54), despite the supervised model having a slightly diffeerent balance between sensitivity and 
specifincity (Table 4). Overall, these results indicate that the semi-supervised approach betteer 
captured the baseline spatial extent of surface water and more faithfully reproduced the 
expected inverse relationship between surface water and bare land.

Discussion
Theis study was designed to evaluate whether satellite imagery can support two related but 
distinct increasingly complex riverscape-monitoring tasks: (1) mapping temporal inundation 
dynamics within the river corridor and (2) predicting hydraulically de finned mesohabitat classes 
directly from image-derived features. Theese targets diffeer in difficculty and intended use. Thee 
inundation analysis tests whether satellite products can reliably track broad surface-water and 
exposed-land dynamics through time, whereas the mesohabitat analysis tests whether finner 
hydraulic habitat categories can be inferred from spectral information across changing floow 
conditions.

===== PAGE 21 =====
For the finrst target, temporal dynamics in inundation, definned as surface water versus exposed 
bare land, were recovered with strong agreement using the semi-supervised pipeline (accuracy 
= 0.84; MCC = 0.66), exceeding the supervised baseline in balanced accuracy and MCC (Table 4; 
Fig. 5-6). Beyond classifincation accuracy, the results demonstrate that satellite-derived products 
capture a coherent temporal exchange between surface water and exposed land, refloecting 
expansion and contraction of wetteed channel margins and in-channel bars through time. Theis 
indicates strong and temporally stable spectral separability of open water at the working scale, 
consistent with the efficcacy of visible–NIR contrasts and water indices for surface-water 
mapping (Jin et al., 2021), while also con finrming that these signals can be leveraged to quantify 
physically meaningful inundation dynamics.
For the second target, direct prediction of mesohabitat labels using a leave-one-date-out design 
yielded variable cross-date performance. Thee supervised model consistently outperformed the 
semi-supervised approach, with mean per-pixel accuracy of approximately 0.67 compared to 
0.31 for the semi-supervised model, and substantial fold-to-fold variability linked to class 
composition and discharge state. Although mesohabitat prediction was more difficcult than 
binary inundation mapping, the prediction maps retained coherent spatial structure, including 
pool-like classes along the thalweg and high-velocity classes near constrictions (Fig. 11). Theis 
suggests that four-band PlanetScope imagery contains a learnable mesohabitat signal, but that 
direct mesohabitat classifincation remains more sensitive to class imbalance, floow dependence, 
and missing hydraulic or morphological context than the simpler inundation task.
Interpretation of Inundation Results
Thee semi-supervised pipeline yielded six clusters that were readily consolidated into finve land-
cover labels. Within the river corridor, impervious and bare substrates were not reliably 
separable spectrally; treating mixed/impervious pixels as “bare land” was appropriate given the 
absence of in-channel infrastructure aside from static bridge decks, which do not affeect 
temporal comparisons. Choice of the cluster count (k) remains subjective and was finxed for 
consistency; a formal sensitivity analysis (varying k, band sets, and date combinations) was not 
undertaken here but would clarify stability versus over-partitioning trade-offes under noisy 
conditions (Vahidi et al., 2023).
Across sensors, the semi-supervised change signal was comparatively insensitive to input 
resolution. PlanetScope at ~3 m captured finner temporal variation in areal change, Sentinel-2 
provided intermediate spatial detail, and Landsat-9 produced directionally consistent patteerns 
of similar magnitude. Theis concordance indicates that rapid, lower-resolution deployments can 
recover robust mesoscale trends, while higher-resolution inputs refinne the amplitude and 
spatial detail of detected changes. A key outcome of this analysis is that the temporal 
relationship between surface water and exposed land is both consistent and physically 
meaningful. Thee expected inverse relationship between these classes was clearly preserved by 
the semi-supervised workfloow, with Kendall’s τ indicating moderate to strong negative 
associations (Landsat-9: τ = -0.64 for temporal change and -0.49 relative to baseline; 
PlanetScope: τ = -0.60 and -0.39, respectively). In contrast, the supervised model exhibited 
weak to negligible relationships (Landsat-9: τ = -0.03 and 0.03; PlanetScope: τ = 0.16 and 0.20), 
indicating reduced sensitivity to hydrologically meaningful change.

===== PAGE 22 =====
Theese results demonstrate that the semi-supervised approach not only classifines surface water 
effeectively, but also captures the underlying hydrologic dynamics governing channel behavior. 
Thee observed temporal exchange between surface water and exposed land refloects the shifteing 
balance between wetteed area and emergent substrates (e.g., bars, shallow margins), which 
definnes the geomorphic template within which mesohabitats form and reorganize. As such, the 
inundation analysis represents a substantive outcome of the study in its own right and 
provides a necessary foundation for interpreting mesohabitat dynamics.
Interpretation of Mesohabitat Results
Given mean per-pixel accuracies of 0.305 and 0.665 for the semi-supervised and supervised 
models, respectively (Fig. 10-11), direct mesohabitat classifincation from four-band PlanetScope 
features remained substantially more difficcult than binary inundation mapping, with the semi-
supervised model showing particularly strong fold-to-fold variability. Theis contrast highlights 
an important distinction: while broad inundation dynamics are spectrally robust and 
temporally consistent, finner hydraulic habitat classes are more sensitive to floow conditions, 
spatial scale, and feature representation.
Theis outcome is consistent with several interacting factors. First, class boundaries are floow-
dependent and nonstationary: mesohabitats translate, expand, and contract with discharge and 
local hydraulics, so a given pixel can change label across dates, penalizing cross-date 
generalization unless hydrodynamic context is reinforced or encoded (Harris et al., 2024; 
Wegscheider et al., 2020). Second, mesohabitat boundaries o fteen occur over short spatial scales 
relative to pixel resolution and are inflouenced by finne-scale hydraulic gradients, leading to 
localized mixing and increased classifincation ambiguity, particularly along rifflee–pool 
transitions. Theird, several mesohabitat distinctions are spectrally ambiguous at four bands 
because they are partly morphodynamic states; much of their separability is carried by surface-
texture and morphological cues (e.g., roughness patteerns, local slope/curvature) not modeled 
here Moortgat et al. (2022).
Class imbalance further limits parameter learning; some classes are sparse or absent in 
particular folds—even with inverse-frequency weighting Ma et al. (2019). Finally, label noise 
arising from image–model mismatch (e.g., minor timing offesets between imagery and hydraulic 
simulations, bathymetric uncertainty, and co-registration drifte) propagates into the categorical 
targets and limits atteainable class-balanced accuracy under stringent cross-date validation. 
Under these constraints, performance remains limited when relying on a purely spectral 
feature set. Nonetheless, the predicted maps exhibit coherent spatial organization, indicating 
the presence of a learnable signal that could be strengthened by incorporating texture metrics 
and morphological context.
Sources of Error and Uncertainty
Theree error modes were most evident: (i) edge confusion at unit boundaries where mixed pixels 
dominate; (ii) systematic bias toward dominant classes in the semi-supervised cluster-to-label 
step when clusters straddle multiple units; and (iii) fold-specifinc shocks when rare classes 
vanish from training or test, producing unstable per-fold metrics. Theese behaviors mirror 
sensitivities reported in object- and pixel-based riverscape mapping where segmentation 
parameters, illumination, and turbidity modulate separability (Demarchi et al., 2016; Kutz et al.,  

===== PAGE 23 =====
2022; Zhang et al., 2023). Uncertainty quanti fincation (e.g., entropy of class probabilities) was not 
computed here but is recommended to floag ambiguous zones for analyst review.
Practical implications
From a monitoring standpoint, the inundation pipeline is deployment-ready for tracking 
wetteed area and corridor dynamics through time. Importantly, this includes not only static 
delineation of surface water, but also quantifincation of temporal exchange between water and 
exposed land, which provides a direct proxy for hydrologic variability and geomorphic activity 
within the channel. Theis study displayed that even low to moderate-resolution, freely available 
sensors such as Landsat-9 and Sentinel-2 provide an effeective balance between spatial detail 
and temporal coverage for operational monitoring. Regardless, the mesohabitat pipeline should 
be treated as proof-of-concept: it reveals where image-only predictors already align with 
hydraulic classes and where additional information is required to meet decision thresholds 
(e.g., habitat suitability modeling, restoration design). Importantly, the results support a tiered 
product strategy: use surface water maps operationally and generate mesohabitat layers where 
data richness permits.
Future considerations
Targeted enhancements are expected to materially improve mesohabitat prediction accuracy:
 Restore native spatial support (~3 m) and add texture metrics (e.g., Gray-Level Co-
occurrence Matrix [GLCM] contrast, entropy, local variance) to encode surface 
roughness and floow streaking (Farwell et al., 2021).
 Incorporate morphological context from DEM-derived slope/curvature or shallow 
bathymetry proxies where available, aligning with evidence that OBIA/terrain fusion 
improves riverscape unit mapping (Demarchi et al., 2016).
 Adopt a hierarchical classifiner (i.e. water to unit family to subclass) to reduce 
confusability among finne classes and stabilize minority categories.
 Explore spatial models (e.g., shallow CNN on PlanetScope tiles or RF with engineered 
texture windows) to inject neighborhood context beyond pixel spectra (Ma et al., 2019; 
Moortgat et al., 2022).
 Use cost-sensitive learning and focal sampling for rare classes; consider active learning 
to prioritize annotation where uncertainty is high (Maskey et al., 2018).
 Where feasible, evaluate hybrid/physics-informed fusion that brings co-registered 
hydraulic predictors (i.e. depth/velocity rasters) alongside imagery, an approach likely 
to raise ceiling performance (Harris et al., 2024).
 Quaantify predictive uncertainty (e.g. calibration), enabling conservative products for 
management use.

Conclusion
Satellite-based workfloows accurately recover inundation within river corridors and, critically, 
capture the temporal dynamics of surface water and exposed land that definne channel behavior 
through time. Theese results demonstrate that satellite imagery can be used not only for static 
mapping, but for quantifying hydrologically meaningful change at the reach scale. Direct 
mesohabitat prediction from four-band PlanetScope features at aggregated resolution yields 
lower and more variable cross-date accuracy than inundation mapping but still exhibits 
meaningful spatial structure, indicating that mesohabitat signal is present and partially 
learnable. Thee path to decision-grade products is clear: use finne spatial resolution where 
possible, enrich features with texture and morphology, adopt hierarchical/imbalance-aware 
learning, and, where available, fuse hydraulics. Together, these steps align with the broader 
trajectory in high-resolution RS and ecohydraulics toward routine, basin-scale mapping of 
mesoscale habitat structure (Moortgat et al., 2022; Verma et al., 2021; Wegscheider et al., 2020).
