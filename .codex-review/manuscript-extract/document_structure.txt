P0018 [Heading1] Abstract
P0019 [FirstParagraph] Mapping dynamic riverine habitats presents a particular challenge, often requiring high resolution bathymetric measurement and advanced sediment transport and hydraulic computational methods. To bypass these resource-intensive requirements, this study establishes a benchmarking framework to assess the limits of high-cadence satellite imagery for riverscape assessment. This study evaluates two open-source workflows for mapping fluvial mesohabitats from optical satellite data: a semi-supervised clustering pipeline and a class-weighted supervised random forest. Increasingly complex targets were tested: (i) inundation dynamics, defined as surface water versus exposed land within the river corridor, and (ii) direct prediction of hydraulically derived mesohabitat classes. Using PlanetScope (~3 m), Sentinel-2 (10–20 m), and Landsat-9 (30 m) imagery co-registered to 2D hydraulic model outputs, the semi-supervised workflow recovered inundation with strong agreement and preserved the expected inverse temporal relationship between surface water and exposed land. Surface-water validation against a baseline mask derived from median 2D hydraulic model mesohabitat extent showed that the semi-supervised model outperformed the supervised baseline on class-balanced metrics, including balanced accuracy and Matthews Correlation Coefficient. In a leave-one-date-out evaluation across seven flow conditions, direct mesohabitat classification from PlanetScope features yielded lower and more variable performance than binary inundation mapping, although the supervised model consistently exceeded the semi-supervised approach and both produced spatially coherent predictions. These results demonstrate that lightweight, open workflows can provide decision-ready inundation products while also capturing hydrologically meaningful temporal dynamics, whereas direct image-based mesohabitat prediction remains a promising but more challenging task. Future gains will likely require retaining native spatial resolution, incorporating texture and morphological context, and integrating hydraulic predictors.

P0021 [Heading1] Introduction
P0022 [BodyText] High-resolution remote sensing (RS) has transformed observation of riverscapes by enabling routine, synoptic mapping of water extent, channel morphology, and habitat structure at organism-relevant spatial grains and operational cadences (Marcus and Fonstad, 2010). Expanding public and commercial image archives, cloud platforms, and open analytical ecosystems now support ecological assessment at spatial and temporal resolutions that field data collection campaigns alone cannot sustain (Cavender-Bares et al., 2022). Field surveys remain indispensable yet are constrained by access, safety, and personnel, particularly during high flows or across difficult floodplain terrain, creating persistent gaps in coverage and frequency (Calderon and An, 2016; Griffin and Lasko, 2020; Johansen et al., 2022; Suir et al., 2023). Moreover, ecohydraulic mapping at scale is constrained by the cost and data demands of LiDAR-driven or fully calibrated hydraulic models (Lindenschmidt and Carr, 2018; Schwartz, 2016). Because mesohabitat composition shifts with streamflow, models must respect flow dependence and test temporal generalization (Aadland, 1993; Harris et al., 2024).
P0023 [FirstParagraph] 
P0024 [BodyText] Within hierarchical river habitat frameworks, mesohabitats (e.g., pools, riffles/glides, runs/raceways) occupy the intermediate scale between microhabitats and reaches and exert first-order control on hydraulics, sediment processes, and aquatic assemblages (Aadland, 1993; Wegscheider et al., 2020). The spatial configuration of these mesohabitats shifts with streamflow and sediment dynamics, highlighting the need for mapping approaches that are both spatially extensive and temporally responsive (Van Rooijen and Lotsari, 2024; Zaimes et al., 2021). Conventional delineation via expert field mapping and ecohydraulic modeling can achieve high fidelity but is typically limited to short river segments and sparse flow snapshots given the demands for detailed topography, boundary conditions, and calibration (Harris et al., 2024; Schwartz, 2016; Wiest et al., 2024).
P0025 [BodyText] Remote sensing provides an opportunity to upscale mesohabitat assessment by exploiting spectral, textural, and morphological signals. Habitat studies are increasingly using RS tools such as supervised/unsupervised land-cover classification, spectral indices (e.g., Normalized Difference Vegetation Index [NDVI], Normalized Difference Water Index [NDWI], Normalized Burn Ratio [NBR]), texture metrics, and Digital Elevation Model (DEM) derived context (e.g. slope, curvature) (Demir et al., 2018; Farwell et al., 2021; Ganesh et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2021; Li et al., 2018; McKay et al., 2022; Niedballa et al., 2022; Suttidate et al., 2023). Riverscape units have been mapped via geographic object-based image analysis (OBIA) by coupling image segmentation, spectral features, and contextual/topographic constraints. Hierarchical, multiscale OBIA that fuses very high resolution (VHR) imagery with LiDAR has produced accurate maps of channels, bars, riparian vegetation, and, in some cases, in-channel mesohabitats (Demarchi et al., 2016, 2016; Kutz et al., 2022; Shabat and Tapamo, 2017). Yet performance can be sensitive to segmentation parameters and scene complexity, with transferability across dates, sites, and flow states often requiring retuning, especially where specular reflection, turbidity, shadows, and narrow channels degrade separability (Wang et al., 2019; Wurm et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023).
P0026 [BodyText] Despite substantial advances in data availability, an operational gap remains: can mesohabitat types be predicted directly from satellite features across streamflow regimes without reliance on site-specific LiDAR or dense field annotation, and evaluated using temporally independent, hydrologically relevant validation? Object-based pipelines often require site/timestep retuning and can degrade under heterogeneous riverscape conditions (i.e. specular reflection, shadows, turbidity), limiting transferability in dynamic fluvial environments (Demarchi et al., 2016, 2016; Kutz et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023).
P0027 [BodyText] Change-detection frameworks further enable assessment of temporal dynamics across seasons and events (Choukiker and Dohare, 2021; Lim et al., 2018; Romero et al., 2016; Zimmermann et al., 2007). Sensor choice is important; medium spatial resolution sensors (5–30 m) incur mixed pixels in narrow channel widths, whereas most VHR (<5 m) constellations trade spatial grain for temporal cadence. PlanetScope (~3 m, near-daily) offers a pragmatic balance for riverscape monitoring and has seen wide uptake across aquatic and terrestrial applications (Planet Labs PBC, 2024). National aerial programs (e.g., National Agriculture Imagery Program [NAIP]; U.S. Geological Survey [USGS] 1-m orthoimagery) provide sub-meter data but at lower or irregular temporal frequency, complicating multi-date analyses tied to flow (USDA, n.d.; USGS, 2023a, 2023b, 2011).
P0028 [BodyText] 
P0029 [BodyText] A large body of work has leveraged geographic object-based image analysis (OBIA) to map riverscape units by coupling image segmentation, spectral features, and contextual/topographic constraints. Hierarchical, multiscale OBIA that fuses very high resolution (VHR) imagery with LiDAR has produced accurate maps of channels, bars, riparian vegetation, and, in some cases, in-channel mesohabitats (Demarchi et al., 2016, 2016; Kutz et al., 2022; Shabat and Tapamo, 2017). Yet performance can be sensitive to segmentation parameters and scene complexity, with transferability across dates, sites, and flow states often requiring retuning, especially where specular reflection, turbidity, shadows, and narrow channels degrade separability (Wang et al., 2019; Wurm et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023).
P0030 [BodyText] Machine learning (ML) and deep learning have expanded capabilities by learning features directly from data. For example, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), classification and representation learning models, and other classical ML models provide robust baselines and ensembles for tabular/image features (Aggarwal, 2018; Howard and Gugger, 2020; Kuhn, 2008; Lantz, 2023; Ma et al., 2019; Thompson and Brodrick, 2021). In fluvial contexts, deep learning networks at sub-meter resolution can exploit texture and spatial context to classify water and hydraulically relevant surface states—even with limited spectral bands—while hybrid workflows integrating image-derived flow texture with terrain cues have distinguished glides, riffles, and pools across discharge conditions (Brostow et al., 2009; Moortgat et al., 2022; Romero et al., 2016; Shashikant, 2019; Verma et al., 2021). These developments suggest that mesohabitat categories may be predictable from imagery when provided appropriate annotations and cross-date validation.
P0031 [BodyText] Sensor choice is important; medium spatial resolution sensors (5–30 m) incur mixed pixels in narrow channel widths, whereas most VHR (<5 m) constellations trade spatial grain for temporal cadence. PlanetScope (~3 m, near-daily) offers a pragmatic balance for riverscape monitoring and has seen wide uptake across aquatic and terrestrial applications (Planet Labs PBC, 2024). National aerial programs (e.g., National Agriculture Imagery Program [NAIP]; U.S. Geological Survey [USGS] 1-m orthoimagery) provide sub-meter data but at lower or irregular temporal frequency, complicating multi-date analyses tied to flow (USDA, n.d.; USGS, 2023a, 2023b, 2011).
P0032 [BodyText] Persistent challenges include assembling labeled datasets at scale, coping with class imbalance (i.e. uneven distribution) among mesohabitat types, and ensuring robustness to domain shifts in color, turbidity, substrate, and illumination (Mandlik and Mundhe, 2018; Maskey et al., 2018; Vahidi et al., 2023, Aggarwal, 2018; Fix and Hodges, 1989; Ma et al., 2019; Maskey et al., 2018; Vahidi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2019; Wurm et al., 2019).). Mesohabitat distributions are typically imbalanced, with dominant units (e.g., runs or raceways) comprising most of the channel, while pools and riffles occupy comparatively limited area (Aadland, 1993). Imbalances biases model training toward majority classes and reducing sensitivity to rarer units. Domain shift further limits transferability, as variations in water optical properties, sediment load, bed composition, channel morphology, and illumination alter spectral responses, causing models trained under one set of conditions to degrade when applied across rivers or flow states (Ma et al., 2019).
P0033 [BodyText] 
P0034 [BodyText] This study advances riverscape mapping by testing whether mesohabitat classes can be inferred directly from high-resolution satellite features across varying discharges, rather than relying on physical parameters (e.g. depth and velocity). Using the Big Blue–Kansas Rivers confluence as a case study, the work (i) establishes reach-scale baseline landcover land-use (LULC) and mesoscale change products for context and continuity, and (ii) evaluates image-based prediction of eight hydraulically defined mesohabitats. The central objective is to quantify how well satellite-only predictors recover mesohabitat labels under temporally out-of-sample conditions and to benchmark those results against the simpler inundation classification (i.e., binary water versus non-water derived from spectral reflectance and indices), delivering categorical prediction maps and cross-date performance summaries suitable for monitoring and management. All analyses are implemented with open, reusable tooling to maximize transparency and transferability.
P0035 [Heading1] Methodology
P0036 [FirstParagraph] This study employs two complementary classification workflows to evaluate riverscape mapping from satellite imagery: (i) a semi-supervised clustering approach (K-means with KNN labeling) and (ii) a supervised random forest classifier. Both methods were applied to satellite imagery with different spatial resolutions to generate landcover and inundation products across the full 22-image time series. These classifications were then used to quantify temporal dynamics in surface water and exposed land within the river corridor. In a separate analysis, image-based predictors derived from PlanetScope imagery were used to directly classify hydraulically defined mesohabitats for seven dates corresponding to 2D hydraulic model outputs, evaluated using a leave-one-date-out framework to assess temporal generalization across flow conditions.
P0037 [Heading2] Software & Data Requirements
P0038 [FirstParagraph] The geospatial processing procedure was completed for the data prior to the statistical analysis utilizing the R statistical language (R Core Team, 2026). A suite of R spatial tools including the terra, in concert with the tidyterra and rgee, were used for the data acquisition steps (Aybar et al., 2020; Hernangómez, 2023; Hijmans et al., 2026). The rgee package is a nested wrapper that connects R to the Google Earth Engine (GEE) Web REST API via the GEE Python API (Aybar et al., 2024; Eltayeb Elmahal and Mahmoud Ibrahim Musa, 2023). The USGS discharge data from the nearest sites on the Kansas River (#6879100 Fort Riley and #6887500 Wamego) and the Big Blue River (#6887000 Manhattan) were downloaded by using the R package dataRetrievel (De Cicco et al., 2023) (Fig. 1b-c). The R package tidymodels was used to employ the ML methods (Kuhn and Wickham, 2020). Polygon drafting and editing was done in the QGIS geographic information system software (QGIS Development Team, 2024).

P0040 [FirstParagraph] The area of interest (AOI) is located at the confluence of the Big Blue and Kansas Rivers in the greater Manhattan, Kansas area (Fig. 1). The analysis domain was restricted to the river corridor by applying a 30-m (98.4252-ft) buffer to the USGS Watershed Boundary Dataset’s (WBD) waterbody layer (USGS, 2023d). The river corridor was subsequently refined to include point bars and other geomorphic features beyond the initial buffer.

P0047 [BodyText] Sentinel-2 (10-20-m) imagery retrieved via GEE (Aybar et al., 2024; European Space Agency, 2023) was included to provide an intermediate spatial resolution dataset that bridges the scale gap between Landsat-9 and PlanetScope (~3 m). Bands were limited to the blue, green, red, and narrow NIR for direct comparison with the other sensors. While the visible bands are provided at 10-m spatial resolution, the narrow NIR band is natively 20 m and was resampled to 10 m using bilinear interpolation to ensure consistent spatial resolution across all predictor layers.
P0048 [BodyText] Because multiple PlanetScope scenes were often available per day, image selection was constrained to those with 0% cloud cover and minimal atmospheric or illumination artifacts; when multiple candidates met these criteria, scenes acquired closest to local solar noon were preferentially selected to reduce shadow effects and ensure radiometric consistency. PlanetScope imagery was limited to the blue, green, red, and NIR bands and consisted of Level-3 surface reflectance products (AnalyticMS_SR), which are atmospherically corrected and provided as analysis-ready reflectance data. The PlanetScope scenes were not always acquired at the same time as the Landsat-9 imagery due to differences in revisit schedules and data availability; however, images were selected to be temporally proximate (i.e., same day or within a 48-hr window) to maintain consistency in surface conditions across datasets. Sentinel-2 imagery followed a similar temporal matching approach, though a slightly wider tolerance window (±11 days) was permitted where necessary.
P0049 [BodyText] The data for the 22 images were combined into three raster stacks corresponding to Landsat-9, Sentinel-2, and PlanetScope. In addition to this multi-temporal dataset used for inundation classification, a second set of PlanetScope images collected between December 22, 2021 and September 28, 2022 corresponding to seven hydrologic conditions was compiled for comparison with modeled aquatic mesohabitat distributions described in the subsequent section.
P0050 [Heading2] Aquatic Mesohabitat Types and Surface-Water Baseline Distribution
P0051 [FirstParagraph] Aquatic mesohabitat types were defined from a 2D HEC-RAS v6.3.1 hydraulic model simulation on a 3-m grid using depth–velocity thresholds consistent with mesoscale typologies (Wiest et al., 2024). The 2D HEC-RAS was calibrated to field-measured water surface elevation to a 0.09m RMSE, approximately 17% of the depth range [Manhattan (#6887000) was at 12 cms at time of data collection, and Wamego (#6887500) at 22 cms, see Wiest et al. 2024 for more information].
P0052 [FirstParagraph] Mesohabitat classes were derived by applying threshold depth and velocity ranges from Aadland (1993) to hydraulic simulation results (e.g., low velocity and greater depth for pools; higher velocity and shallow depth for riffles). Classes comprised (1) shallow pool, (2) medium pool, (3) deep pool, (4) slow riffle, (5) fast riffle, (6) raceway, (7) faster than a raceway, and (8) faster than a deep pool (Table 2). Seven hydrologic snapshots spanning the site’s discharge regime were selected from USGS mean-daily flow records from nearby Fort Riley (#6879100), Manhattan (#6887000), and Wamego (#6887500) stations via the dataRetrieval package (De Cicco et al., 2023): December 22, 2021; February 12, 2022; April 17, 2022; May 31, 2022; June 20, 2022; July 18, 2022; and September 28, 2022 (Fig. 2).

P0057 [Heading2] ML Classification
P0058 [Heading3] Semi-Supervised Classification
P0059 [FirstParagraph] A semi-supervised classification approach was used to categorize the imagery into five classes based off the Anderson land cover classification (Anderson et al., 1976): urban (e.g. roads, buildings, etc), bare land, herbaceous vegetation (e.g., grasses, agricultural cover), forested land, and surface water. This approach was applied consistently across sensors to enable direct comparison.
P0060 [BodyText] The workflow combined unsupervised clustering with supervised classification. K-means clustering (Hartigan–Wong algorithm) was first used to partition the spectral feature space by minimizing within-cluster sum of squares (Hartigan and Wong, 1979). The resulting clusters were then assigned class labels and used to train a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier, which predicts class membership for unseen pixels based on the majority class of neighboring observations in feature space.
P0061 [BodyText] The number of clusters (k) was evaluated using both the elbow method and silhouette analysis. The elbow method identifies the point of diminishing returns in within-cluster variance reduction, while silhouette analysis evaluates cluster separation by comparing within- and between-cluster similarity. Although silhouette analysis indicated optimal clustering at low k values (k = 2–3), these solutions primarily separated broad landcover groups and did not capture the level of thematic detail intended for this study. Therefore, an elbow at a higher value of k (> 5) was selected based on a balance between statistical performance and interpretability of landcover classes.
P0062 [Heading3] Supervised Classification
P0063 [FirstParagraph] A supervised classification approach was used to assign pixels to the five landcover classes described in the previous section. Landcover classes were manually delineated as vector polygons within the AOI using QGIS (QGIS Development Team, 2024) and subsequently rasterized to generate labeled training pixels. To reduce temporal noise in the training data, only areas that appeared visually stable across the study period (i.e., exhibited no observable landcover change between dates) were selected.
P0064 [BodyText] Model training was conducted using a random forest classifier, with 80% of the labeled pixels used for training and the remaining 20% reserved for validation. The number of trees was fixed at 500 for all models. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using five-fold cross-validation on a stratified subset of the training data, where a maximum of 10,000 samples per class were randomly selected to reduce computational demand while preserving class balance. The optimal model configuration was selected based on classification performance metrics, including overall accuracy and Receiver Operating Characteristic Area Under the Curve (ROC AUC). Two key hyperparameters were tuned: (1) min_n, the minimum number of observations required to split a node, and (2) mtry, the number of predictors randomly sampled at each split.
P0065 [BodyText] The final models were trained at their original spatial resolutions using the full set of labeled pixels and the tuned hyperparameters. For the baseline classification, predictor variables consisted of multi-band raster stacks in which each pixel contained spectral information from all image acquisition dates for a given sensor. This formulation allows the model to learn a temporally integrated representation of surface conditions, producing a classification that reflects the dominant landcover state over the study period.
P0066 [BodyText] In addition, separate random forest models were trained for each image date using only the spectral bands associated with that date. These date-specific models preserve short-term variability in landcover conditions and were used to generate temporally explicit classified rasters. Because the baseline model was trained using a multi-band stack containing predictors from all image dates, the tuned mtry reflects a high-dimensional feature space. In contrast, the date-specific models were trained using only four spectral predictors per date. As a result, mtry was constrained to 2 for the date-specific models to ensure consistency with the reduced predictor set.
P0067 [Heading2] Time Series Analysis
P0068 [FirstParagraph] Although five landcover classes were defined to characterize general surface conditions, the time series analyses focused on surface water and bare land within the river corridor. Urban, herbaceous vegetation, and bare land substrates were not reliably separable spectral categories and were therefore grouped as “bare land,” while riparian trees and shrubs classes were retained for completeness but not emphasized in downstream change analyses.
P0069 [BodyText] The classified rasters generated from both the semi-supervised (K-means + KNN) and supervised (random forest) workflows—hereafter referred to as ML-classified rasters—were masked to the river corridor to limit noise from surrounding landcover. For each date, class-specific area was calculated by multiplying the number of pixels assigned to each class by the pixel area (1 m² = 0.00024711 acres) and summing across the AOI.

P0074 [BodyText] To further evaluate relationships between landcover classes, Kendall’s tau () rank correlation coefficient was computed between surface water and bare land area time series. Kendall’s is a nonparametric measure of monotonic association that is robust to non-normality and small sample sizes. It is calculated based on the difference between concordant and discordant pairs of observations and ranges from -1 to +1, where negative values indicate an inverse relationship, positive values indicate a direct relationship, and values near zero indicate little or no association. In this study, Kendall’s was used to assess whether increases in surface water correspond to decreases in exposed bare land, as expected under varying flow conditions.
P0075 [Heading2] Model Evaluation
P0076 [Heading3] Surface Water Validation (Water vs. Non-Water)
P0077 [FirstParagraph] For the water versus non-water validation, a baseline surface-water mask was derived from the seven 2D hydraulic model–derived mesohabitat maps by taking the per-pixel median mesohabitat state across dates. The dates were specifically chosen to represent a range of diverse flow conditions, resulting in changing flow volumes and different mesohabitat distributions across time. Pixels within the median mesohabitat footprint were classified as water, whereas pixels outside this footprint were classified as non-water.
P0078 [BodyText] PlanetScope imagery acquired on the same seven dates was co-registered to the hydraulic grid and used as input to the semi-supervised and supervised classification workflows. The resulting classified products were then evaluated against the baseline mask using confusion matrices. This analysis was designed to assess whether image-based classifications could recover the spatial extent of surface water consistent with hydraulically derived conditions.
P0079 [BodyText] Model performance was summarized using overall accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, negative predictive value, balanced accuracy, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and F-measure. These metrics were used to evaluate both overall agreement and the ability of each model to recover surface water under class imbalance, with particular emphasis on balanced accuracy and MCC because they are less sensitive to unequal class frequencies than overall accuracy alone.
P0080 [Heading3] Mesohabitat Classification
P0081 [FirstParagraph] To evaluate whether mesohabitat types can be predicted directly from satellite data, rather than inferred indirectly through inundation, 3-m resolution 2D hydraulic model–derived mesohabitat distribution maps were used as categorical ground truth, and image-based models were trained to predict seven aquatic mesohabitat types described in Section 2.3. PlanetScope imagery corresponding to the seven hydraulic model dates specified in Section 2.3 was co-registered to the mesohabitat grid.
P0082 [BodyText] Predictors were restricted to PlanetScope imagery for this analysis. Landsat-9 and Sentinel-2 data were not used because they did not provide consistent, cloud-free coverage for all seven hydraulic model dates and introduced mixed-pixel effects at 30 m resolution that are unsuitable for resolving narrow channel features. Inclusion of Landsat-9 and Sentinel-2 would have required either dropping dates or resampling mesohabitat labels to a lower resolution to match the coarser spatial resolution of the two sensors.
P0083 [BodyText] A leave-one-date-out (LOO) design was adopted to account for the flow dependence of mesohabitats. For each iteration, one date was held out for testing while models were trained on the remaining six dates and evaluated on the hold-out. Two modeling approaches were compared. The first approach used semi-supervised clustering. Predictor variables were standardized, and K-means clustering was applied to the training pixels ( = 12; nstart = 10). Each cluster was assigned a mesohabitat label based on majority voting from the training data, and predictions for the held-out date were generated by assigning pixels to the nearest cluster center in standardized feature space. The second approach used a supervised random forest classifier. A class-weighted model with the same hyperparameters described in Section 2.4.2 was implemented using the R tidymodels and ranger packages. Class weights were defined as inversely proportional to class frequency in the training data to mitigate imbalance. Predictor variables included the four spectral bands and three derived indices.
P0084 [BodyText] All modeling and evaluation were restricted to the mesohabitat mask (i.e., the river corridor). Model performance was summarized for each fold using overall accuracy and class-balanced metrics (balanced accuracy and macro-averaged recall), and prediction maps for each held-out date were exported as categorical rasters.
P0085 [Heading1] Results
P0086 [Heading2] Optimized Modeling Parameters
P0087 [FirstParagraph] The optimized modeling parameters for the unsupervised and semi-supervised methods were determined using the elbow method and random forest hyperparameters, respectively. The elbow method indicated diminishing improvements in within-cluster variance reduction beyond ≈ 6–10 (Fig. 3). Silhouette analysis identified = 6 as the optimal solution, yielding the highest average silhouette width; this value was therefore adopted for subsequent modeling. The K-means algorithm was run with a maximum of 500 iterations and applied to the full image collection to establish baseline landcover conditions. Random forest hyperparameter tuning indicated that performance was consistently high across a broad range of parameter combinations, with AUC values exceeding 0.9998 for both PlanetScope and Landsat-9 datasets (Fig. 4). P0092 [Heading2] Model Summary and Performance
P0093 [Heading3] Surface Water Validation
P0094 [FirstParagraph] Five land-cover classes were mapped (i.e. urban, bare land, herbaceous vegetation, riparian trees/shrubs, and surface water); however, the analysis focused on surface water and bare land as complementary indicators of inundation dynamics (Fig. 5–6]). For this analysis, the bare land class includes pixels classified as urban and herbaceous vegetation. The remaining classes were included to support classification but are not considered further in the validation.
P0095 [BodyText] Predicted surface water and bare land extents varied across sensors and modeling approaches (Fig. 7). For Landsat-9, surface water spanned approximately 183–390 acres under the semi-supervised model and 249–329 acres under the supervised model, while bare land ranged from 372–623 and 268–488 acres, respectively. For Sentinel-2, surface water ranged from 311–386 acres (semi-supervised) and 146–332 acres (supervised), with bare land ranging from 111–277 and 241–495 acres. PlanetScope showed surface water extents of 161–382 acres for the semi-supervised model and 294–341 acres for the supervised model, and bare land ranging from 120–241 and 206–288 acres, respectively.
P0096 [BodyText] The temporal relationship between surface water and bare land remained inverse across sensors and modeling approaches (Figs. 8, 9). For Landsat-9, Kendall’s tau values were negative for both semi-supervised and supervised classifications, ranging from =-0.73 to -0.50. The semi-supervised model yielded =-0.73 for both between-date change and deviations relative to baseline, whereas the supervised model produced =-0.55 and -0.50, respectively. Sentinel-2 also showed negative associations for both approaches. The semi-supervised model yielded =-0.60 for between-date change and =-0.37 relative to baseline, while the supervised model produced =-0.73 and -0.51, respectively. PlanetScope likewise showed negative relationships between surface water and bare land. The semi-supervised model yielded =-0.58 for between-date change and =-0.49 relative to baseline, whereas the supervised model produced =-0.60 and -0.62, respectively. Across all three sensors, Kendall’s tau remained negative for both between-date changes and baseline-relative deviations, indicating an inverse temporal relationship between surface water and bare land.
P0097 [BodyText] The median mesohabitat distribution derived from the seven 2D hydraulic model dates defined the baseline surface-water footprint used for validation (Fig. 11). Confusion-matrix results showed that the semi-supervised model identified more true surface water pixels than the supervised model (304 vs. 235), but also produced more false positives (43 vs. 23; Table 3). The supervised model more frequently classified non-water correctly (183 vs. 163), but also failed to detect a larger number of true water pixels (115 false negatives vs. 46 for the semi-supervised model). Consistent with these patterns, the semi-supervised model achieved higher balanced accuracy (0.66) and MCC (0.66) than the supervised model (balanced accuracy = 0.56; MCC = 0.54), despite the supervised model having a slightly different balance between sensitivity and specificity (Table 4). Overall, these results indicate that the semi-supervised approach better captured the baseline spatial extent of surface water and more faithfully reproduced the expected inverse relationship between surface water and bare land.

P0112 [Heading3] Mesohabitat Label Prediction
P0113 [FirstParagraph] The LOO evaluation across seven dates demonstrates consistently higher performance for the supervised model relative to the semi-supervised approach in contrast to the landcover classification results (Fig. 10). Mean per-pixel accuracy (± SD) was 0.305 ± 0.19 for the semi-supervised model and 0.665 ± 0.05 for the supervised model. Balanced accuracy and macro-averaged F1 exhibited similar patterns, with the supervised model maintaining stable performance across all dates, while the semi-supervised model showed greater variability and consistently lower values. Performance varied across folds, particularly for the semi-supervised model (accuracy range: 0.076–0.668), reflecting sensitivity to changes in hydraulic conditions and class distributions. In contrast, the supervised model achieved relatively consistent accuracy (0.605–0.761) and balanced accuracy (0.628–0.660), indicating stronger generalization across temporal variability (Fig. 10).
P0114 [BodyText] Spatial comparisons further illustrate these differences (Fig. 11). The supervised model reproduces the overall spatial organization of mesohabitats, including channel-scale structure and transitions between pool and high-velocity units, although predictions exhibit increased pixel-level noise relative to the observed maps. The semi-supervised model captures broad spatial patterns in some cases but shows reduced class diversity and a tendency toward class collapse in others, particularly under low or high flow conditions.

P0119 [Heading1] Discussion
P0120 [FirstParagraph] This study was designed to evaluate whether satellite imagery can support increasingly complex riverscape-monitoring tasks: (1) mapping temporal inundation dynamics within the river corridor and (2) predicting hydraulically defined mesohabitat classes directly from image-derived features.
P0121 [BodyText] For the first target, temporal dynamics in inundation, defined as surface water versus exposed bare land, were recovered with strong agreement using the semi-supervised pipeline (accuracy = 0.84; MCC = 0.66), exceeding the supervised baseline in balanced accuracy and MCC (Table 4; Fig. 5-6). Beyond classification accuracy, the results demonstrate that satellite-derived products capture a coherent temporal exchange between surface water and exposed land, reflecting expansion and contraction of wetted channel margins and in-channel bars through time. This indicates strong and temporally stable spectral separability of open water at the working scale, consistent with the efficacy of visible–NIR contrasts and water indices for surface-water mapping (Jin et al., 2021), while also confirming that these signals can be leveraged to quantify physically meaningful inundation dynamics.
P0122 [BodyText] For the second target, direct prediction of mesohabitat labels using a leave-one-date-out design yielded variable cross-date performance. The supervised model consistently outperformed the semi-supervised approach, with mean per-pixel accuracy of approximately 0.67 compared to 0.31 for the semi-supervised model, and substantial fold-to-fold variability linked to class composition and discharge state. Although mesohabitat prediction was more difficult than binary inundation mapping, the prediction maps retained coherent spatial structure, including pool-like classes along the thalweg and high-velocity classes near constrictions (Fig. 11). This suggests that four-band PlanetScope imagery contains a learnable mesohabitat signal, but that direct mesohabitat classification remains more sensitive to class imbalance, flow dependence, and missing hydraulic or morphological context than the simpler inundation task.
P0123 [Heading2] Interpretation of Inundation Results
P0124 [FirstParagraph] The semi-supervised pipeline yielded six clusters that were readily consolidated into five land-cover labels. Within the river corridor, impervious and bare substrates were not reliably separable spectrally; treating mixed/impervious pixels as “bare land” was appropriate given the absence of in-channel infrastructure aside from static bridge decks, which do not affect temporal comparisons. Choice of the cluster count (k) remains subjective and was fixed for consistency; a formal sensitivity analysis (varying k, band sets, and date combinations) was not undertaken here but would clarify stability versus over-partitioning trade-offs under noisy conditions (Vahidi et al., 2023).
P0125 [BodyText] Across sensors, the semi-supervised change signal was comparatively insensitive to input resolution. PlanetScope at ~3 m captured finer temporal variation in areal change, Sentinel-2 provided intermediate spatial detail, and Landsat-9 produced directionally consistent patterns of similar magnitude. This concordance indicates that rapid, lower-resolution deployments can recover robust mesoscale trends, while higher-resolution inputs refine the amplitude and spatial detail of detected changes. A key outcome of this analysis is that the temporal relationship between surface water and exposed land is both consistent and physically meaningful. The expected inverse relationship between these classes was clearly preserved by the semi-supervised workflow, with Kendall’s indicating moderate to strong negative associations (Landsat-9: = -0.64 for temporal change and -0.49 relative to baseline; PlanetScope: = -0.60 and -0.39, respectively). In contrast, the supervised model exhibited weak to negligible relationships (Landsat-9: = -0.03 and 0.03; PlanetScope: = 0.16 and 0.20), indicating reduced sensitivity to hydrologically meaningful change.
P0126 [BodyText] These results demonstrate that the semi-supervised approach not only classifies surface water effectively, but also captures the underlying hydrologic dynamics governing channel behavior. The observed temporal exchange between surface water and exposed land reflects the shifting balance between wetted area and emergent substrates (e.g., bars, shallow margins), which defines the geomorphic template within which mesohabitats form and reorganize. As such, the inundation analysis represents a substantive outcome of the study in its own right and provides a necessary foundation for interpreting mesohabitat dynamics.
P0127 [Heading2] Interpretation of Mesohabitat Results
P0128 [FirstParagraph] Given mean per-pixel accuracies of 0.305 and 0.665 for the semi-supervised and supervised models, respectively (Fig. 10-11), direct mesohabitat classification from four-band PlanetScope features remained substantially more difficult than binary inundation mapping, with the semi-supervised model showing particularly strong fold-to-fold variability. This contrast highlights an important distinction: while broad inundation dynamics are spectrally robust and temporally consistent, finer hydraulic habitat classes are more sensitive to flow conditions, spatial scale, and feature representation.
P0129 [BodyText] This outcome is consistent with several interacting factors. First, class boundaries are flow-dependent and nonstationary: mesohabitats translate, expand, and contract with discharge and local hydraulics, so a given pixel can change label across dates, penalizing cross-date generalization unless hydrodynamic context is reinforced or encoded (Harris et al., 2024; Wegscheider et al., 2020). Second, mesohabitat boundaries often occur over short spatial scales relative to pixel resolution and are influenced by fine-scale hydraulic gradients, leading to localized mixing and increased classification ambiguity, particularly along riffle–pool transitions. Third, several mesohabitat distinctions are spectrally ambiguous at four bands because they are partly morphodynamic states; much of their separability is carried by surface-texture and morphological cues (e.g., roughness patterns, local slope/curvature) not modeled here Moortgat et al. (2022).
P0130 [BodyText] Class imbalance further limits parameter learning; some classes are sparse or absent in particular folds—even with inverse-frequency weighting Ma et al. (2019). Finally, label noise arising from image–model mismatch (e.g., minor timing offsets between imagery and hydraulic simulations, bathymetric uncertainty, and co-registration drift) propagates into the categorical targets and limits attainable class-balanced accuracy under stringent cross-date validation. Under these constraints, performance remains limited when relying on a purely spectral feature set. Nonetheless, the predicted maps exhibit coherent spatial organization, indicating the presence of a learnable signal that could be strengthened by incorporating texture metrics and morphological context.
P0131 [Heading2] Sources of Error and Uncertainty
P0132 [FirstParagraph] Three error modes were most evident: (i) edge confusion at unit boundaries where mixed pixels dominate; (ii) systematic bias toward dominant classes in the semi-supervised cluster-to-label step when clusters straddle multiple units; and (iii) fold-specific shocks when rare classes vanish from training or test, producing unstable per-fold metrics. These behaviors mirror sensitivities reported in object- and pixel-based riverscape mapping where segmentation parameters, illumination, and turbidity modulate separability (Demarchi et al., 2016; Kutz et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). Uncertainty quantification (e.g., entropy of class probabilities) was not computed here but is recommended to flag ambiguous zones for analyst review.
P0133 [Heading2] Practical implications
P0134 [FirstParagraph] From a monitoring standpoint, the inundation pipeline is deployment-ready for tracking wetted area and corridor dynamics through time. Importantly, this includes not only static delineation of surface water, but also quantification of temporal exchange between water and exposed land, which provides a direct proxy for hydrologic variability and geomorphic activity within the channel. This study displayed that even low to moderate-resolution, freely available sensors such as Landsat-9 and Sentinel-2 provide an effective balance between spatial detail and temporal coverage for operational monitoring. Regardless, the mesohabitat pipeline should be treated as proof-of-concept: it reveals where image-only predictors already align with hydraulic classes and where additional information is required to meet decision thresholds (e.g., habitat suitability modeling, restoration design). Importantly, the results support a tiered product strategy: use surface water maps operationally and generate mesohabitat layers where data richness permits.
P0135 [Heading2] Future considerations
P0136 [FirstParagraph] Targeted enhancements are expected to materially improve mesohabitat prediction accuracy:
P0137 [-] Restore native spatial support (~3 m) and add texture metrics (e.g., Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix [GLCM] contrast, entropy, local variance) to encode surface roughness and flow streaking (Farwell et al., 2021).
P0138 [-] Incorporate morphological context from DEM-derived slope/curvature or shallow bathymetry proxies where available, aligning with evidence that OBIA/terrain fusion improves riverscape unit mapping (Demarchi et al., 2016).
P0139 [-] Adopt a hierarchical classifier (i.e. water to unit family to subclass) to reduce confusability among fine classes and stabilize minority categories.
P0140 [-] Explore spatial models (e.g., shallow CNN on PlanetScope tiles or RF with engineered texture windows) to inject neighborhood context beyond pixel spectra (Ma et al., 2019; Moortgat et al., 2022).
P0141 [-] Use cost-sensitive learning and focal sampling for rare classes; consider active learning to prioritize annotation where uncertainty is high (Maskey et al., 2018).
P0142 [-] Where feasible, evaluate hybrid/physics-informed fusion that brings co-registered hydraulic predictors (i.e. depth/velocity rasters) alongside imagery, an approach likely to raise ceiling performance (Harris et al., 2024).
P0143 [-] Quantify predictive uncertainty (e.g. calibration), enabling conservative products for management use.
P0144 [Heading2] Conclusion
P0145 [FirstParagraph] Satellite-based workflows accurately recover inundation within river corridors and, critically, capture the temporal dynamics of surface water and exposed land that define channel behavior through time. These results demonstrate that satellite imagery can be used not only for static mapping, but for quantifying hydrologically meaningful change at the reach scale. Direct mesohabitat prediction from four-band PlanetScope features at aggregated resolution yields lower and more variable cross-date accuracy than inundation mapping but still exhibits meaningful spatial structure, indicating that mesohabitat signal is present and partially learnable. The path to decision-grade products is clear: use fine spatial resolution where possible, enrich features with texture and morphology, adopt hierarchical/imbalance-aware learning, and, where available, fuse hydraulics. Together, these steps align with the broader trajectory in high-resolution RS and ecohydraulics toward routine, basin-scale mapping of mesoscale habitat structure (Moortgat et al., 2022; Verma et al., 2021; Wegscheider et al., 2020).
