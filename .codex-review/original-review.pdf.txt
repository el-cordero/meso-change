Abstract34
High-cadence satellite imagery creates new opportunities for riverscape assessment35
when methods are reproducible, efficient, and robust to changing flow conditions. This study36
evaluates two open-source workflows for mapping fluvial mesohabitats from optical satellite37
data: a semi-supervised clustering pipeline and a class-weighted supervised random forest.38
Two complementary targets were considered: (i) inundation dynamics, defined as surface39
water versus exposed land within the river corridor, and (ii) direct prediction of hydraulically40
derived mesohabitat classes. Using PlanetScope (~3 m), Sentinel-2 (10–20 m), and Landsat-941
(30 m) imagery co-registered to HEC-RAS outputs, the semi-supervised workflow recovered42
inundation with strong agreement and preserved the expected inverse temporal relationship43
between surface water and exposed land. Surface-water validation against a baseline mask44
derived from median HEC-RAS mesohabitat extent showed that the semi-supervised model45
outperformed the supervised baseline on class-balanced metrics, including balanced accuracy46
and Matthews Correlation Coefficient. In a leave-one-date-out evaluation across seven flow47
conditions, direct mesohabitat classification from PlanetScope features yielded lower and48
more variable performance than binary inundation mapping, although the supervised model49
consistently exceeded the semi-supervised approach and both produced spatially coherent50
predictions. These results demonstrate that lightweight, open workflows can provide decision-51
ready inundation products while also capturing hydrologically meaningful temporal dynamics,52
whereas direct image-based mesohabitat prediction remains a promising but more challenging53
task. Future gains will likely require retaining native spatial resolution, incorporating texture54
and morphological context, and integrating hydraulic predictors.55

1 Introduction58
High-resolution remote sensing (RS) has transformed observation of riverscapes by59
enabling routine, synoptic mapping of water extent, channel morphology, and habitat structure60
at organism-relevant spatial grains and operational cadences (Marcus and Fonstad, 2010).61
Expanding public and commercial image archives, cloud platforms, and open analytical62
ecosystems now support ecological assessment at spatial and temporal resolutions that field63
data collection campaigns alone cannot sustain (Cavender-Bares et al., 2022). Field surveys64
remain indispensable yet are constrained by access, safety, and personnel, particularly during65
high flows or across difficult floodplain terrain, creating persistent gaps in coverage and66
frequency (Calderon and An, 2016; Griffin and Lasko, 2020; Johansen et al., 2022; Suir et al.,67
2023).68
Within hierarchical river habitat frameworks, mesohabitats (e.g., pools, riffles/glides,69
runs/raceways) occupy the intermediate scale between microhabitats and reaches and exert70
first-order control on hydraulics, sediment processes, and aquatic assemblages (Aadland,71
1993; Wegscheider et al., 2020). The spatial configuration of these mesohabitats shifts with72
discharge and sediment dynamics, highlighting the need for mapping approaches that are73

both spatially extensive and temporally responsive (Van Rooijen and Lotsari, 2024; Zaimes et74
al., 2021). Conventional delineation via expert field mapping and ecohydraulic modeling can75
achieve high fidelity but is typically limited to short river segments and sparse flow snapshots76
given the demands for detailed topography, boundary conditions, and calibration (Harris et77
al., 2024; Schwartz, 2016; Wiest et al., 2024).78
Remote sensing provides an opportunity to upscale mesohabitat assessment by exploiting79
spectral, textural, and morphological signals. Habitat studies are increasingly using RS tools80
such as supervised/unsupervised land-cover classification, spectral indices (e.g., Normalized81
Difference Vegetation Index [NDVI], Normalized Difference Water Index [NDWI], Normalized82
Burn Ratio [NBR]), texture metrics, and Digital Elevation Model (DEM) derived context83
(e.g. slope, curvature) (Demir et al., 2018; Farwell et al., 2021; Ganesh et al., 2023; Jin et84
al., 2021; Li et al., 2018; McKay et al., 2022; Niedballa et al., 2022; Suttidate et al., 2023).85
Change-detection frameworks further enable assessment of temporal dynamics across seasons86
and events (Choukiker and Dohare, 2021; Lim et al., 2018; Romero et al., 2016; Zimmermann87
et al., 2007).88
A large body of work has leveraged geographic object-based image analysis (OBIA)89
to map riverscape units by coupling image segmentation, spectral features, and contex-90
tual/topographic constraints. Hierarchical, multiscale OBIA that fuses very high resolution91
(VHR) imagery with LiDAR has produced accurate maps of channels, bars, riparian vege-92
tation, and, in some cases, in-channel mesohabitats (Demarchi et al., 2016, 2016; Kutz et93
al., 2022; Shabat and Tapamo, 2017). Yet performance can be sensitive to segmentation94
parameters and scene complexity, with transferability across dates, sites, and flow states95
often requiring retuning, especially where specular reflection, turbidity, shadows, and narrow96
channels degrade separability (Wang et al., 2019; Wurm et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023).97
Machine learning (ML) and deep learning have expanded capabilities by learning features98
directly from data. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), classification and representation99
learning models; and other classical ML models provide robust baselines and ensembles for100
tabular/image features (Aggarwal, 2018; Howard and Gugger, 2020; Kuhn, 2008; Lantz,101
2023; Ma et al., 2019; Thompson and Brodrick, 2021). In fluvial contexts, deep learning102
networks at sub-meter resolution can exploit texture and spatial context to classify water103
and hydraulically relevant surface states—even with limited spectral bands—while hybrid104
workflows integrating image-derived flow texture with terrain cues have distinguished glides,105
riffles, and pools across discharge conditions (Brostow et al., 2009; Moortgat et al., 2022;106
Romero et al., 2016; Shashikant, 2019; Verma et al., 2021). These developments suggest107
that mesohabitat categories may be predictable directly from imagery, provided appropriate108
annotations and cross-date validation.109
Sensor choice is important; medium spatial resolution sensors (5–30 m) incur mixed110
pixels in narrow channel widths, whereas most VHR (<5 m) constellations trade spatial grain111
against temporal cadence. PlanetScope (~3 m, near-daily) offers a pragmatic balance for112
riverscape monitoring and has seen wide uptake across aquatic and terrestrial applications113
(Planet Labs PBC, 2024). National aerial programs (e.g., National Agriculture Imagery114
Program [NAIP]; U.S. Geological Survey [USGS] 1-m orthoimagery) provide sub-meter data115
but at lower or irregular temporal frequency, complicating multi-date analyses tied to flow116

(USDA, n.d.; USGS, 2023a, 2023b, 2011).117
Persistent challenges include assembling labeled datasets at scale, coping with class118
imbalance among mesohabitats, and ensuring robustness to domain shifts in color, turbidity,119
substrate, and illumination (Mandlik and Mundhe, 2018; Maskey et al., 2018; Vahidi et al.,120
2023). Mesohabitat distributions are typically imbalanced, with dominant units (e.g., runs121
or raceways) comprising most of the channel, while pools and riffles occupy comparatively122
limited area, biasing model training toward majority classes and reducing sensitivity to rarer123
units (Aadland, 1993). Domain shift further limits transferability, as variations in water124
optical properties, sediment load, bed composition, channel morphology, and illumination125
alter spectral responses, causing models trained under one set of conditions to degrade when126
applied across rivers or flow states (Ma et al., 2019).127
Despite substantial advances, an operational gap remains: can mesohabitat types128
be predicted directly from satellite features across streamflow regimes without reliance on129
site-specific LiDAR or dense field annotation, and evaluated using temporally independent,130
hydrologically relevant validation? Object-based pipelines often require site/timestep retuning131
and can degrade under heterogeneous riverscape conditions (i.e. specular reflection, shadows,132
turbidity), limiting transferability (Demarchi et al., 2016, 2016; Kutz et al., 2022; Zhang et al.,133
2023). Deep learning and modern ML offer stronger representation capacity but remain sen-134
sitive to label scarcity, class imbalance, and domain shift across color/turbidity/illumination135
(Aggarwal, 2018; Fix and Hodges, 1989; Ma et al., 2019; Maskey et al., 2018; Vahidi et al.,136
2023; Wang et al., 2019; Wurm et al., 2019). Moreover, ecohydraulic mapping at scale is137
constrained by the cost and data demands of LiDAR-driven or fully calibrated hydraulic138
models (Lindenschmidt and Carr, 2018; Schwartz, 2016). Because mesohabitat composition139
shifts with discharge, models must respect flow dependence and test temporal generalization140
(Aadland, 1993; Harris et al., 2024). These considerations motivate a label-efficient, imagery-141
first strategy that exploits high-cadence VHR sources (e.g., PlanetScope) for multi-date142
coverage while limiting mixed-pixel issues typical of coarser sensors in narrow channel widths143
(e.g. Planet Labs PBC, 2024; USGS, 2023c).144
This study advances riverscape mapping by testing whether mesohabitat classes can145
be inferred directly from high-resolution satellite features across varying discharges, rather146
than relying on inundation proxies. Focusing on the Big Blue–Kansas Rivers confluence147
(Manhattan, KS), the work (i) establishes reach-scale baseline landcover land-use (LULC)148
and mesoscale change products for context and continuity, and (ii) evaluates image-based149
prediction of eight hydraulically defined mesohabitats using PlanetScope imagery (Planet150
Labs PBC, 2024) due to its consistent, near-same-day coverage of all modelled dates. The151
central objective is to quantify how well satellite-only predictors recover mesohabitat labels152
under temporally out-of-sample conditions and to benchmark those results against the simpler153
inundation classification (i.e., binary water versus non-water derived from spectral reflectance154
and indices), delivering categorical prediction maps and cross-date performance summaries155
suitable for monitoring and management. All analyses are implemented with open, reusable156
tooling to maximize transparency and transferability.157

2 Methodology158
This study employs two complementary classification workflows to evaluate riverscape159
mapping from satellite imagery: (i) a semi-supervised clustering approach (K-means with160
KNN labeling) and (ii) a supervised random forest classifier. Both methods were applied161
to multi-date Landsat-9 (30 m), Sentinel-2 (10-20 m) and PlanetScope (~3 m) imagery to162
generate landcover and inundation products across the full 22-image time series. These163
classifications were then used to quantify temporal dynamics in surface water and exposed164
land within the river corridor. In a separate analysis, image-based predictors derived from165
PlanetScope imagery were used to directly classify hydraulically defined mesohabitats for166
seven dates corresponding to HEC-RAS model outputs, evaluated using a leave-one-date-out167
framework to assess temporal generalization across flow conditions.168

2.4 ML Classification246
2.4.1 Semi-Supervised Classification247
A semi-supervised classification approach was used to categorize the imagery into248
five classes based off the Anderson land cover classification (Anderson et al., 1976): urban249
(e.g. roads, buildings, etc), bare land, herbaceous vegetation (e.g., grasses, agricultural cover),250
8

===== PAGE 9 =====
forested land, and surface water. This approach was applied consistently across sensors to251
enable direct comparison across sensors.252
The workflow combined unsupervised clustering with supervised classification. K-means253
clustering (Hartigan–Wong algorithm) was first used to partition the spectral feature space by254
minimizing within-cluster sum of squares (Hartigan and Wong, 1979). The resulting clusters255
were then assigned class labels and used to train a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier,256
which predicts class membership for unseen pixels based on the majority class of neighboring257
observations in feature space.258
The number of clusters (k) was evaluated using both the elbow method and silhouette259
analysis. The elbow method identifies the point of diminishing returns in within-cluster260
variance reduction, while silhouette analysis evaluates cluster separation by comparing within-261
and between-cluster similarity. Although silhouette analysis indicated optimal clustering at262
low k values (k = 2–3), these solutions primarily separated broad landcover groups and did263
not capture the level of thematic detail required for this study. Therefore, higher values of k264
(> 5) are preferred based on a balance between statistical performance and interpretability of265
landcover classes.266
2.4.2 Supervised Classification267
A supervised classification approach was used to assign pixels to the five landcover268
classes described in the previous section. Landcover classes were manually delineated as vector269
polygons within the AOI using QGIS (QGIS Development Team, 2024) and subsequently270
rasterized to generate labeled training pixels. To reduce temporal noise in the training data,271
only areas that appeared visually stable across the study period (i.e., exhibited no observable272
landcover change between dates) were selected.273
Model training was conducted using a random forest classifier, with 80% of the labeled274
pixels used for training and the remaining 20% reserved for validation. The number of trees275
was fixed at 500 for all models. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using five-fold cross-276
validation on a stratified subset of the training data, where a maximum of 10,000 samples277
per class were randomly selected to reduce computational demand while preserving class278
balance. The optimal model configuration was selected based on classification performance279
metrics, including overall accuracy and Receiver Operating Characteristic Area Under the280
Curve (ROC AUC). Two key hyperparameters were tuned: (1)min_n, the minimum number281
of observations required to split a node, and (2)mtry, the number of predictors randomly282
sampled at each split.283
The final models were trained at their original spatial resolutions using the full set of284
labeled pixels and the tuned hyperparameters. For the baseline classification, predictor vari-285
ables consisted of multi-band raster stacks in which each pixel contained spectral information286
from all image acquisition dates for a given sensor. This formulation allows the model to287
learn a temporally integrated representation of surface conditions, producing a classification288
that reflects the dominant or persistent landcover state over the study period.289
In addition, separate random forest models were trained for each image date using290
only the spectral bands associated with that date. These date-specific models preserve291

short-term variability in landcover conditions and were used to generate temporally explicit292
classified rasters. Because the baseline model was trained using a multi-band stack containing293
predictors from all image dates, the tuned mtry reflects a high-dimensional feature space. In294
contrast, the date-specific models were trained using only four spectral predictors per date.295
As a result,mtrywas constrained to 2 for the date-specific models to ensure consistency with296
the reduced predictor set.297
2.5 Time Series Analysis298
Although five landcover classes were defined to characterize general surface conditions,299
the analyses focused on surface water and bare land within the river corridor. Urban,300
herbaceous vegetation, and bare land substrates were not reliably separable spectral categories301
and were therefore grouped as “bare land,” while riparian trees and shrubs classes were302
retained for completeness but not emphasized in downstream change analyses.303
The classified rasters generated from both the semi-supervised (K-means + KNN) and304
supervised (random forest) workflows—hereafter referred to as ML-classified rasters—were305
masked to the river corridor to limit noise from surrounding landcover. For each date,306
class-specific area was calculated by multiplying the number of pixels assigned to each class307
by the pixel area (1 m2 = 0.00024711 acres) and summing across the AOI.308

2.6 Model Evaluation325
2.6.1 Surface Water Validation (Water vs. Non-Water)326
For the water versus non-water validation, a baseline surface-water mask was derived327
from the seven HEC-RAS–derived mesohabitat maps by taking the per-pixel median meso-328
habitat state across dates. Pixels within the median mesohabitat footprint were classified as329
water, whereas pixels outside this footprint were classified as non-water.330
PlanetScope imagery acquired on the same seven dates was co-registered to the hydraulic331
grid and used as input to the semi-supervised and supervised classification workflows. The332
resulting classified products were then evaluated against the baseline mask using confusion333
matrices. This analysis was designed to assess whether image-based classifications could334
recover the spatial extent of surface water consistent with hydraulically derived conditions.335
Model performance was summarized using overall accuracy, sensitivity, specificity,336
precision, negative predictive value, balanced accuracy, Matthews Correlation Coefficient337
(MCC), and F-measure. These metrics were used to evaluate both overall agreement and338
the ability of each model to recover surface water under class imbalance, with particular339
emphasis on balanced accuracy and MCC because they are less sensitive to unequal class340
frequencies than overall accuracy alone.341
2.6.2 Mesohabitat Classification342
To evaluate whether mesohabitat types can be predicted directly from satellite data,343
rather than inferred indirectly through inundation, 3-m resolution HEC-RAS–derived meso-344
habitat distribution maps were used as categorical ground truth, and image-based models345
were trained to predict seven aquatic mesohabitat types described in Section 2.3. Plan-346
etScope imagery corresponding to the seven hydraulic model dates specified in Section 2.3347
was co-registered to the mesohabitat grid.348
Predictors were restricted to PlanetScope imagery for this analysis. Landsat-9 and349
Sentinel-2 data were not used because they did not provide consistent, cloud-free coverage for350
all seven hydraulic model dates and introduced mixed-pixel effects at 30 m resolution that are351
unsuitable for resolving narrow channel features. Inclusion of Landsat-9 and Sentinel-2 would352
have required either dropping dates or resampling mesohabitat labels to a lower resolution to353
match the coarser spatial resolution of the two sensors.354
A leave-one-date-out (LOO) design was adopted to account for the flow dependence355
of mesohabitats. For each iteration, one date was held out for testing while models were356
trained on the remaining six dates and evaluated on the hold-out. Two modeling approaches357
were compared. The first approach used semi-supervised clustering. Predictor variables were358
standardized, and K-means clustering was applied to the training pixels (k = 12; nstart359
= 10). Each cluster was assigned a mesohabitat label based on majority voting from the360
training data, and predictions for the held-out date were generated by assigning pixels to the361
nearest cluster center in standardized feature space. The second approach used a supervised362
random forest classifier. A class-weighted model with the same hyperparameters described in363
Section 2.4.2 was implemented using the Rtidymodelsandrangerpackages. Class weights364

4 Discussion448
4.1 Summary of Principal Findings449
Two complementary targets were evaluated. First, temporal dynamics in inundation450
(surface water versus exposed land) within the river corridor were quantified and recovered451
with strong agreement using the semi-supervised pipeline (accuracy = 0.84; MCC = 0.66),452
exceeding the supervised baseline in balanced accuracy and MCC (Table 4; Fig. 5-6). Beyond453
classification accuracy, the results demonstrate that satellite-derived products capture a454
coherent temporal exchange between surface water and exposed land, reflecting expansion455
and contraction of wetted channel margins and in-channel bars through time. This indicates456
strongandtemporallystablespectralseparabilityofopenwaterattheworkingscale, consistent457
with the efficacy of visible–NIR contrasts and water indices for surface-water mapping (Jin et458
al., 2021), while also confirming that these signals can be leveraged to quantify physically459
meaningful inundation dynamics.460
Second, direct prediction of mesohabitat labels (Shallow/Medium/Deep Pool; Slow/Fast461
Riffle; Raceway; Faster-than-a-Raceway; Faster-than-a-Deep-Pool) using a leave-one-date-462
out design yielded variable cross-date performance, with the supervised model consistently463
outperforming the semi-supervised approach (mean per-pixel accuracy≈ 0.31 and 0.67,464
respectively), and with substantial fold-to-fold variability linked to class composition and465
discharge state. Despite the lower and more variable absolute metrics for mesohabitat466
prediction relative to inundation mapping, prediction maps show coherent spatial structure467
(e.g., pool-like classes along the thalweg; high-velocity classes near constrictions; Fig. 11),468
indicating a learnable signal from four-band PlanetScope features even after aggregation.469
4.2 Interpretation of Inundation Results470
The semi-supervised pipeline yielded six clusters that were readily consolidated into five471
land-cover labels. Within the river corridor, impervious and bare substrates were not reliably472
separable spectrally; treating mixed/impervious pixels as “bare land” was appropriate given473
the absence of in-channel infrastructure aside from static bridge decks, which do not affect474
temporal comparisons. Choice of the cluster count (k) remains subjective and was fixed for475
consistency; a formal sensitivity analysis (varying k, band sets, and date combinations) was476
not undertaken here but would clarify stability versus over-partitioning trade-offs under noisy477
conditions (Vahidi et al., 2023).478
Across sensors, the semi-supervised change signal was comparatively insensitive to input479
resolution. PlanetScope at ~3 m captured finer temporal variation in areal change, Sentinel-480
2 provided intermediate spatial detail, and Landsat-9 produced directionally consistent481
patterns of similar magnitude. This concordance indicates that rapid, lower-resolution482
deployments can recover robust mesoscale trends, while higher-resolution inputs refine the483
amplitude and spatial detail of detected changes. A key outcome of this analysis is that484
the temporal relationship between surface water and exposed land is both consistent and485
physically meaningful. The expected inverse relationship between these classes was clearly486
preserved by the semi-supervised workflow, with Kendall’sτindicating moderate to strong487

These results demonstrate that the semi-supervised approach not only classifies surface492

4.3 Interpretation of Mesohabitat Results499
Given mean per-pixel accuracies of 0.305 and 0.665 for the semi-supervised and su-500
pervised models, respectively (Fig. 10-11), direct mesohabitat classification from four-band501
PlanetScope features remained substantially more difficult than binary inundation mapping,502
with the semi-supervised model showing particularly strong fold-to-fold variability. This503
contrast highlights an important distinction: while broad inundation dynamics are spectrally504
robust and temporally consistent, finer hydraulic habitat classes are more sensitive to flow505
conditions, spatial scale, and feature representation.506
This outcome is consistent with several interacting factors. First, class boundaries507
are flow-dependent and nonstationary: mesohabitats translate, expand, and contract with508
discharge and local hydraulics, so a given pixel can change label across dates, penalizing509
cross-date generalization unless hydrodynamic context is encoded Harris et al. (2024). Second,510
mesohabitat boundaries often occur over short spatial scales relative to pixel resolution and511
are influenced by fine-scale hydraulic gradients, leading to localized mixing and increased512
classification ambiguity, particularly along riffle–pool transitions. Third, several mesohabitat513
distinctions are spectrally ambiguous at four bands because they are partly morphodynamic514
states; much of their separability is carried by surface-texture and morphological cues (e.g.,515
roughness patterns, local slope/curvature) not modeled here Moortgat et al. (2022).516
Class imbalance further limits parameter learning; some classes are sparse or absent517
in particular folds—even with inverse-frequency weighting Ma et al. (2019). Finally, label518
noise arising from image–model mismatch (e.g., minor timing offsets between imagery and519
hydraulic simulations, bathymetric uncertainty, and co-registration drift) propagates into the520
categorical targets and limits attainable class-balanced accuracy under stringent cross-date521
validation. Under these constraints, performance remains limited when relying on a purely522
spectral feature set. Nonetheless, the predicted maps exhibit coherent spatial organization,523
indicating the presence of a learnable signal that could be strengthened by incorporating524
texture metrics and morphological context.525

4.4 Sources of Error and Uncertainty526
Three error modes were most evident: (i) edge confusion at unit boundaries where527
mixed pixels dominate; (ii) systematic bias toward dominant classes in the semi-supervised528
cluster-to-label step when clusters straddle multiple units; and (iii) fold-specific shocks529
when rare classes vanish from training or test, producing unstable per-fold metrics. These530
behaviors mirror sensitivities reported in object- and pixel-based riverscape mapping where531
segmentation parameters, illumination, and turbidity modulate separability (Demarchi et532
al., 2016; Kutz et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). Uncertainty quantification (e.g., entropy of533
class probabilities) was not computed here but is recommended to flag ambiguous zones for534
analyst review.535
4.5 Practical implications536
From a monitoring standpoint, the inundation pipeline is deployment-ready for tracking537
wetted area and corridor dynamics through time. Importantly, this includes not only static538
delineation of surface water, but also quantification of temporal exchange between water and539
exposed land, which provides a direct proxy for hydrologic variability and geomorphic activity540
within the channel. This study displayed that even low to moderate-resolution, freely available541
sensors such as Landsat-9 and Sentinel-2 provide an effective balance between spatial detail542
and temporal coverage for operational monitoring. Regardless, the mesohabitat pipeline543
should be treated as proof-of-concept: it reveals where image-only predictors already align544
with hydraulic classes and where additional information is required to meet decision thresholds545
(e.g., habitat suitability modeling, restoration design). Importantly, the results support a546
tiered product strategy: use surface water maps operationally and generate mesohabitat547
layers where data richness permits.548
4.6 Future considerations549
Targeted enhancements are expected to materially improve mesohabitat prediction550
accuracy:551
-Restore native spatial support (~3 m) and add texture metrics (e.g., Gray-Level Co-552
occurrence Matrix [GLCM] contrast, entropy, local variance) to encode surface roughness and553
flow streaking (Farwell et al., 2021). -Incorporate morphological context from DEM-derived554
slope/curvature or shallow bathymetry proxies where available, aligning with evidence that555
OBIA/terrain fusion improves riverscape unit mapping (Demarchi et al., 2016). -Adopt a556
hierarchical classifier (i.e. water to unit family to subclass) to reduce confusability among557
fine classes and stabilize minority categories. -Explore spatial models (e.g., shallow CNN on558
PlanetScope tiles or RF with engineered texture windows) to inject neighborhood context559
beyond pixel spectra (Ma et al., 2019; Moortgat et al., 2022). -Use cost-sensitive learning560
and focal sampling for rare classes; consider active learning to prioritize annotation where561
uncertainty is high (Maskey et al., 2018). -Where feasible, evaluate hybrid/physics-informed562
fusion that brings co-registered hydraulic predictors (i.e. depth/velocity rasters) alongside563
imagery, an approach likely to raise ceiling performance (Harris et al., 2024). -Quantify564
predictive uncertainty (e.g. calibration), enabling conservative products for management use.565

4.7 Conclusion566
Satellite-based workflows accurately recover inundation within river corridors and,567
critically, capture the temporal dynamics of surface water and exposed land that define568
channel behavior through time. These results demonstrate that satellite imagery can be used569
not only for static mapping, but for quantifying hydrologically meaningful change at the570
reach scale. Direct mesohabitat prediction from four-band PlanetScope features at aggregated571
resolution yields lower and more variable cross-date accuracy than inundation mapping but572
still exhibits meaningful spatial structure, indicating that mesohabitat signal is present and573
partially learnable. The path to decision-grade products is clear: use fine spatial resolution574
where possible, enrich features with texture and morphology, adopt hierarchical/imbalance-575
aware learning, and, where available, fuse hydraulics. Together, these steps align with576
the broader trajectory in high-resolution RS and ecohydraulics toward routine, basin-scale577
mapping of mesoscale habitat structure (Moortgat et al., 2022; Verma et al., 2021; Wegscheider578
et al., 2020).579
